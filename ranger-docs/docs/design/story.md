# Story

The main plot spine for the hack. Drafted 2026-04-07. Region-level setting is captured separately in [setting.md](setting.md) (region: **Ardenna**; the wound: **Xerneas dormancy**). Many proper nouns (Company name, CEO, mentor, station names) are still open.

> See also: [Setting (Ardenna)](setting.md), [Tone & Story Arc](../vision/tone-and-arc.md), [Pillars](../vision/pillars.md).

## One-line pitch

A Ranger recruit graduates into a Corps that has been quietly captured from within by a beloved corporation whose ideology is the central thesis of Pokémon canon, industrialized — and whose welfare programs are real, sincere, and killing Pokémon at the operational edge.

## The shape

```
Act 1: wholesome ranger academy ─┐
                                  ├── the turn (single unflinching scene) ──▶ Act 3: muted, grieving, careful
Act 2: missions, escalation ─────┘
```

The opening earns the contrast. Act 3 is not grimdark — it is a *desaturated* version of the same world. Sunlight still hits the ranger base, just at a different angle. The tutorial music doesn't play anymore.

## The factions

### The Ranger Corps

Genuinely good, genuinely captured. Most rangers don't know. Some leaders are bought, some blackmailed, some fed bad intel, most are doing the job they signed up for. The institution is real and sincere — that's why the capture works and why it hurts.

The Corps was *philosophically defenseless* against the Company from day one, because the Corps's foundational belief (the human–Pokémon bond is sacred) is the same belief the Company built its industrial logic on top of. The capture was a logical operation, not a hostile one.

### The Company

A beloved, infrastructure-level corporate presence. The villain — but not visibly, not in act 1, and not on its own terms ever.

**Aesthetic:** soft greens, rounded sans-serif, an NGO-sounding name. The visual opposite of Team Rocket / Magma / Aqua. The player's pattern-matching brain should refuse to file them as villains on sight.

**Legitimacy saturation:** the Company is *load-bearing* for things the player materially benefits from. Their logo is on the first-aid kit in the starter pack. They co-fund the Pokémon Center analog. They sponsor the academy. A friendly NPC works for them. Players don't suspect infrastructure.

**Ideology — welfare-washed labor, drawn from the central thesis of Pokémon canon itself:**

> Pokémon and humans are better together. The bond is real, sacred, and makes Pokémon happier, stronger, and longer-lived than they'd be in the wild. Every game in the series has been telling you this for thirty years. The Company simply takes that truth seriously enough to *scale* it — to extend the gift of partnership beyond the lucky few children who happen to start a journey at age ten, to every Pokémon in the region, as a matter of fairness.

This is not a corruption of canon. It *is* canon, industrialized. The player cannot disagree with the premise without disagreeing with the entire series. Modeled directly on real-world welfare-washing arguments for greyhound racing, horse racing, sled dogs, and working-animal industries — *they love to work, they want purpose, idleness is suffering* — and the rebuttal is the same one those arguments get in real life: the framework was wrong from the start, and everyone inside it is measuring the right things by the wrong definition.

**The horror is not that the Company is lying.** Their welfare officers are sincere. Their public reports are accurate. Their internal metrics are met. The cruelty is only visible at the operational edge, and only to Pokémon, who can't testify. A Machoke develops repetitive-strain injuries no wild Machoke ever would. A Wailmer "logistics partner" hauls cargo on a route that used to be a migration path, and the migration just stopped, and no one comments because the route is more efficient now.

**What they want:** resource and land extraction, with Pokémon as the means of production. Forced labor dressed up as partnership. The land hollows out as collateral.

### The ex-rangers

A small cell — 2–3 named NPCs in v1, with room to grow post-turn — of former rangers who saw the corruption early, were branded eco-terrorists by a coordinated Corps/Company smear campaign, and have done real harm in their fight. The label is not a slander invented for this game; it is what the Company calls them, and it is also approximately correct.

**In act 1 they are presented as straightforward antagonists** in the Team Rocket slot. They steal Pokémon. They blow up Company surveying equipment. They torch a (Company-funded) research outpost. They rough up a sympathetic NPC. The Corps's condemnation is *factually correct* — these people are dangerous, they have hurt people, the player has every reason to chase them. The Company offers a bounty. The player collects it.

**After the turn** the player learns:

- They were right about the diagnosis.
- The Pokémon they "stole" were rescues from Company sites.
- The "sabotage" was the only language the Company would hear.
- The NPC they roughed up was complicit, and they were trying to extract information.
- The Corps and the Company *coordinated* to brand them criminals — one of the conspiracy's earliest visible moves, and the player participated in it without knowing.
- Some of them know exactly what they've done and carry it. Some have hardened past caring.

This is the Andor / *The East* / *Night in the Woods* register: radicalization as a tragedy that produced people who are simultaneously correct and broken. They are not "secretly good guys." Their position is the only coherent counter to the Company's ideology — *the bond is real and sacred and therefore cannot be scaled, systematized, or institutionalized* — and that position is almost mystical and politically useless, which is why they ended up with bombs.

### The player

A new recruit. Their starter is a **gift from the Company** at graduation, as part of the academy's "partner program." Healthy, happy, clearly bonded. In the finale, the CEO points at it. The player has no clean answer.

The player's arc:

1. Recruit at the academy (warm, earnest, low-stakes)
2. Trusted ranger running real missions (warmer still — these are good people doing good work)
3. Unwitting instrument of the Company (the player commits the crime, see below)
4. Discovery, through their own past actions, of what they've been part of
5. Navigation of a world where no existing faction is clean
6. Becoming the nucleus of something new — neither captured nor radicalized

## Craft commitments

These are the techniques the story is committed to. They are non-negotiable; everything else is execution detail. Each is a load-bearing structural choice, not a stylistic preference.

### 1. Legitimacy saturation

The Company must be the reason something the player materially benefits from exists. Candidates: healing infrastructure, the starter pack, fast-travel, the academy itself. Players don't suspect infrastructure.

### 2. Discredit the truth-tellers early

The first NPCs who badmouth the Company should be coded as *wrong about other things* — paranoid, rude, factually incorrect about something the player can verify. The player files their critique under "crank." In act 3, they remember.

### 3. The CEO out-argues the mentor on screen

In act 1, stage a public conversation where the CEO and a senior Ranger debate Pokémon welfare — and the CEO makes the *better* argument, in the player's view. Self-aware, conceding, articulate. The player should leave that scene thinking "huh, the Company is actually pretty thoughtful." Models: N (BW), the Illusive Man (ME2).

### 4. Coherent ideology, sincerely held

The Company's pitch is a real philosophy a kind person could hold (see "The Company" above). It is not a lie. Sympathetic NPCs believe it. The mentor half-believes it. Some welfare officers fully believe it, and are *good at their jobs* by the metrics they were trained on.

### 5. Compartmentalization, not hypocrisy

Complicit NPCs are not lying — they are not connecting two thoughts. The kindly quartermaster genuinely loves the recruits *and* genuinely files paperwork shipping "decommissioned" Pokémon to extraction sites. He has never let himself put the two together. When the player figures it out, they should feel **grief for him, not betrayal by him.**

Rules for these characters:

- **Never have them lie on screen in act 1.** Omission only. A caught lie reads as mustache-twirling; a caught omission reads as tragedy.
- Give each one a small unexplained interior cost in act 1 (drinks too much, sleeps at the office, photo of someone absent). Don't explain it. The player will, later.
- Split the cast: some break and help post-turn; some double down; **one or two never find out and remain warm to the end.** The variance is what proves the warmth was always real.

### 6. The player commits the crime

Drawn from BioShock's "would you kindly." An early, wholesome, tutorial-adjacent mission has the recruit "relocating a dangerous wild population away from a populated area." In act 3 the player returns to that exact map and finds an extraction site where those Pokémon used to live. *The player did this.* No villain in the scene. No monologue. The world tells them.

The hunt for the eco-terrorist cell in act 1 is the *second* instance of this pattern.

### 7. Staggered three-beat reveal

The turn is not one scene. It is three:

1. **Beat 1:** one mission feels off. Deniable. Atmosphere only.
2. **Beat 2:** a pattern across missions. Undeniable but abstract. Probably a document, or an inventory discrepancy, or a return visit to a familiar map.
3. **Beat 3 — the unflinching scene:** a face the player loves is in a photograph they shouldn't be in, and the scene that follows is the *one moment* the game does not soften. See "Tone control" below.

Each beat lets the player feel smart for catching it before the next.

### 8. The Company never reveals itself

The world reveals it. The player's own actions reveal it. A grieving complicit NPC reveals it. **The Company keeps smiling through the credits.** The CEO is still warm, still on screen, still making the better argument *after* the player knows. The dissonance — him meaning it, the player knowing — is the actual horror.

Even in the finale confrontation, the CEO does not break character. He wins the argument on points and acts anyway. The player wins by *acting*, not by out-arguing him.

### 9. Fair-play foreshadowing

Clues are placed in the same frame as louder distractions, for the attentive player to catch on first run and the casual player to catch on replay. Concrete techniques to apply opportunistically:

- The Company logo on something *incongruous* (a surveying tool in a Ranger's pack) rather than only on wallpaper
- A polite "restoration in progress" fence in act 1 → strip mine in act 3
- A tutorial classmate who asks the uncomfortable question and gets gently redirected. Don't make them right yet. Make them *present*.
- Act-1 NPC praise phrased in the grammar of debt and obligation ("everything they've *given* us") rather than affection
- Geographic foreshadowing: act-3 atrocity sites are act-1 hike destinations
- One inventory/dex/roster number that doesn't add up but is never flagged

### 10. The starter is the CEO's strongest argument

The player's starter is a Company-gifted partner. In the finale, the CEO points at it and asks: *what is its name? when did you last let it out of its ball? does it love you? then what, exactly, is your objection?* The player has no clean rhetorical answer. The answer is in the bodies, not the words.

## Tone control

**Act 1:** unguarded warmth. The wholesomeness must be earned and felt, not winked at. No foreshadowing winks to camera. (See [Tone & Story Arc](../vision/tone-and-arc.md).)

**Act 2:** the same warmth, with the three-beat reveal layered underneath. Most players are still having a good time. The attentive ones are starting to feel uneasy without being able to say why.

**The turn (Beat 3):** *one* unflinching scene. Earned, deliberate, never repeated. The game trusts the player to remember it for the rest of the runtime. Models: *Made in Abyss* season 1 finale, the Mother 3 chapter 1 ending, the Pathologic register.

**Act 3:** desaturated, not black. Mother 3 / late *Outer Wilds* / post-Aerith *FF7*. NPCs who used to greet you warmly now nod. The academy still has students. Sunlight still hits the ranger base, just at a different angle. The tutorial music is gone. **Small recovered warmths in act 3** (a complicit NPC who breaks and helps; a moment with the starter the CEO's argument hasn't fully poisoned; a wild population the Company hasn't reached) hit ten times harder against the muted backdrop than they would against unbroken brightness.

**The finale:** the CEO does not break character. He wins on points. The player wins by acting. The credits are quiet.

## What this story does NOT do

- **No casual mode / no difficulty select.** (Pillar.) The story's emotional weight depends on the player having earned the act 1 warmth through real play.
- **No mechanical Ranger capture system.** (Pillar.) Narrative reskin only.
- **No "secretly good guys" reveal for the eco-terrorists.** They are correct *and* dangerous. The game does not let the player off the hook.
- **No villain monologue at the turn.** No found diary, no overheard plot exposition, no captured-and-explains. Information the player did not earn is information the story did not deliver.
- **No second act-1 antagonist faction beyond the eco-terrorist cell.** Scope discipline. The Company is the villain; the cell is the act-1 misdirect.
- **No clean faction in act 3.** The Corps is captured, the Company is the villain, the cell is broken. The player builds the right answer; they do not join it.
- **No grimdark act 3.** Desaturated, grieving, careful. The warmth carries weight; it does not disappear.
